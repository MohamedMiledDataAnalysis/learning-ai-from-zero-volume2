# 2. Risk factors

An investment in the notes involves risk. Prospective investors should read this section together with the
rest of this prospectus, including the transaction overview in section 1, the description of the notes in
section 4, the terms and conditions of the notes in section 5 (the "Conditions"), the description of the
portfolio in section 10, the priority of payments in section 15 and the reserve fund and trigger events in
section 16, before making any decision to invest. The risk factors below are those which [[issuer]] (the
"Issuer") believes to be material to the notes, organised in five groups: risks relating to the notes and the
structure of the transaction; risks relating to the loans; legal and regulatory risks; counterparty risks; and
macroeconomic and market risks. The order in which the risk factors appear is not an indication of the
likelihood of any risk occurring or of the extent of its consequences.

The Issuer believes that the risk factors described below represent the principal risks inherent in investing
in the Notes, but it does not represent that they are exhaustive, and the inability of the Issuer to pay interest,
principal or other amounts on the Notes may occur for other reasons. Prospective investors should reach their own
views, with their own legal, tax, accounting, regulatory and financial advisers, before making an investment
decision.

In this section, the class A notes, the class B notes, the class C notes and the class D notes are referred to
together as the "Notes" and each as a "Class"; the holders of the Notes are the "Noteholders". [[originator]]
(the "Originator", the "Seller" and, in its capacity as servicer, the "Servicer") originated each of the hire
purchase agreements (the "HP Agreements") and personal contract purchase agreements (the "PCP Agreements" and,
together with the HP Agreements, the "Loan Agreements" or the "loans") in the portfolio, and the individuals who
are party to them as customers are the "Borrowers". The motor vehicles financed under the Loan Agreements are the
"Vehicles". The agreements under which the Issuer purchases the loans, appoints the Servicer, Larkmead Bank plc
(in its capacities as cash manager, the "Cash Manager", as issuer account bank, the "Account Bank", as agent bank,
the "Agent Bank", and as principal paying agent, the "Principal Paying Agent")
and the other transaction parties, and grants security for the Notes are the "Transaction Documents"; they are
described in sections 3, 13 and 14. The note trustee and security trustee is referred to as the "Trustee". Terms
used in this section and not defined here have the meanings given to them in section 11 and in the index of
defined terms at the end of this prospectus.

## 2.1 Risks relating to the Notes and the structure of the transaction

**The Notes are limited recourse obligations of the Issuer only, and Noteholders will have no claim against any
other person.**

The Notes are obligations of the Issuer alone. They are not obligations of, and are not guaranteed by, the
Originator, the Servicer, the Trustee, the Cash Manager, the Account Bank, Pellam Corporate Services Ltd
(the "Corporate Services Provider" and the "Back-up Servicer Facilitator"), Corvane Securities plc (the
"Arranger" and the "Lead Manager"), Thames Auto Receivables Holdings Ltd ("Holdings") or any other person. No
person other than the Issuer accepts any liability whatsoever to Noteholders in respect of any failure by the
Issuer to pay any amount due under the Notes, and none of the persons named above has any obligation to provide
funds to the Issuer or to make good any shortfall in the amounts available to the Issuer.

The Issuer's obligations under the Notes are limited recourse. Noteholders' claims are limited to the amounts
which the Trustee receives from the property charged by the Issuer under the deed of charge (the "Deed of
Charge"), which comprises principally the Issuer's interest in the loans and their collections, its rights under
the Transaction Documents and its rights in the issuer accounts described in section 14 (together, the "Charged
Property"). If the Charged Property has been realised and the net proceeds of realisation, after payment of all
amounts ranking in priority to or equally with a Class of Notes, are insufficient to pay that Class in full, the
Issuer will have no further liability for the shortfall, the Noteholders' claims for that shortfall will be
extinguished, and no Noteholder will be entitled to take any further step against the Issuer to recover it.

The Conditions and the Transaction Documents also contain non-petition provisions under which no Noteholder and
no other secured creditor may institute, or join in instituting, insolvency or winding-up proceedings against
the Issuer, or take any other step to enforce the security, other than through the Trustee. Noteholders should
therefore be aware that their only recourse is to the Charged Property through the Trustee, and that the value of
that recourse depends entirely on the performance of the loans and of the transaction parties described in this
section.

**The Issuer's ability to pay the Notes depends entirely on collections from the loans and on the performance of
third parties.**

The Issuer is a special purpose company whose only business is the purchase of the loans, the issue of the Notes
and the entry into the Transaction Documents. It has no employees and no assets other than the Charged Property
and its share capital, and it does not have, and will not have, any significant source of funds other than the
collections on the loans, interest earned on the balances of the issuer accounts and the amounts standing to the
credit of the reserve fund (the "Reserve Fund"). The Available Funds (as defined in section 11) available on each
payment date (each a "Payment Date") to pay interest and principal on the Notes therefore depend on the timely
payment by Borrowers of the amounts due under the Loan Agreements, on the amounts recovered from defaulted loans
and handed-back Vehicles, and on the timely transfer of collections by the Servicer to the Issuer.

There can be no assurance that the Available Funds, together with any amount drawn from the Reserve Fund, will on
any Payment Date be sufficient to pay all interest due on the Notes and to reduce the balance of the Notes in line
with the Pool Balance, nor that on the Final Maturity Date (as defined below) or on any earlier date of
redemption the Charged Property will be sufficient to redeem the Notes in full. The Issuer depends on the
Servicer to collect the loans, on the Cash Manager to calculate and apply the Available Funds in accordance with
section 15, on the Account Bank to hold its cash, and on the Corporate Services Provider to administer its
corporate affairs. A failure by any of them to perform its obligations may reduce or delay payments on the Notes,
as described in section 2.4.

**The junior Classes of Notes are subordinated to the more senior Classes and bear losses first.**

The Notes are constituted by a trust deed (the "Trust Deed") and rank as set out in the priority of payments in
section 15. Interest on the class B notes is paid only after interest on the class A notes has been paid in full;
interest on the class C notes is paid only after interest on the class A notes and the class B notes; and interest
on the class D notes is paid only after interest on all the more senior Classes. After a Trigger Event, principal
is also paid sequentially, in the order class A, class B, class C and class D, as described in section 4. The
subordination of the junior Classes is the principal form of credit enhancement for the more senior Classes, in
addition to the excess spread and the Reserve Fund described below.

As a consequence, a holder of a junior Class bears a greater risk of loss than a holder of a more senior Class.
If losses on the loans exceed the excess spread available to absorb them, the resulting Principal Deficiency (as
described in section 15) is borne in practice first by the class D notes, then by the class C notes, then by the
class B notes and only then by the class A notes, because Available Funds and, on enforcement, the proceeds of the
Charged Property are applied in order of seniority. Holders of the class D notes in particular should be aware
that a relatively small increase in defaults above the levels assumed in section 17, or a relatively small fall in
recovery proceeds, may result in a loss of part or all of their investment, and that interest on their Notes is
paid only from the funds remaining after all more senior items have been paid on the relevant Payment Date.

The relative share of each Class in the total balance of the Notes, set out in section 4, determines the amount
of subordination available to each Class at the Closing Date. That amount will change over the life of the
transaction, as described in the next risk factor. There is no guarantee that the subordination available to any
Class will be sufficient to protect it against the losses which actually arise on the portfolio.

**While principal is paid pro rata, the credit enhancement of the senior Classes does not increase, and after the
switch to sequential payment the junior Classes may be repaid much later than expected.**

Until a Trigger Event has occurred, principal is allocated between all Classes in proportion to their balances, as
described in section 4. During that period, each Class amortises at the same rate, so the percentage of the Note
balance represented by the junior Classes, and therefore the subordination available to the more senior Classes,
remains broadly constant rather than increasing as it would in a fully sequential structure. At the same time the
absolute amount of subordination falls as the junior Classes are repaid. If the performance of the portfolio
deteriorates late in the life of the transaction, when a large part of the junior Classes has already been
repaid, the absolute amount of loss which the junior Classes can absorb will be smaller than it was at the Closing
Date. The cumulative default, arrears and Reserve Fund tests described in section 16 are designed to switch the
transaction to sequential payment before that situation arises, but there can be no assurance that a Trigger
Event will occur early enough, or that the tests will capture every form of deterioration. A deterioration in
residual value performance on handed-back PCP Vehicles, for example, reduces recoveries without necessarily
causing a breach of the arrears test.

Once a Trigger Event has occurred, principal is paid sequentially from the following Payment Date and the switch
is permanent, even if the portfolio performance later recovers and the relevant test would be met again. From
that date the class B notes, the class C notes and the class D notes will receive no principal until each more
senior Class has been repaid in full. The weighted average lives of the junior Classes may therefore extend
materially beyond the figures shown in section 17, and holders of those Classes will be exposed to the credit
performance of the portfolio for longer. Conversely, the class A notes may be repaid more quickly than expected.

Noteholders should also note that the occurrence of a Trigger Event depends on figures calculated by the Servicer
and the Cash Manager from the Servicer's records. Errors in those records or calculations, or a change in the
Servicer's arrears management practices (for example, more extensive use of payment arrangements, which may
reduce the reported arrears without improving the collections), may delay or accelerate the switch to sequential
payment relative to the underlying economic performance of the loans.

**Interest and principal collections are applied in a single priority of payments, so losses and shortfalls in
interest can be met from principal collections and cause a Principal Deficiency.**

The transaction does not separate revenue and principal collections. The Available Funds for each collection
period (each a "Collection Period") comprise interest collections, scheduled principal (including the Balloon
Payments due under PCP Agreements, as described in section 2.2), prepayments and recoveries, and they are applied in the single order set out in
section 15. Fees, expenses and interest on all Classes rank ahead of the payment of principal. The practical
effect is that, if interest collections in a Collection Period are insufficient to pay the senior fees and the
interest on all Classes, principal collections received in that Collection Period will be used to pay those
amounts before any amount is applied to reduce the balance of the Notes.

When that occurs, or when loans default and leave the Pool Balance without a corresponding receipt of cash, the
balance of the Notes remains above the Pool Balance after the Payment Date and a Principal Deficiency arises, as
described in section 15. A Principal Deficiency is reduced on later Payment Dates only from the Available Funds
remaining after the Reserve Fund has been replenished. If the excess spread of the portfolio is insufficient to
eliminate it, the Principal Deficiency will remain outstanding and, at the Final Maturity Date or on an earlier
redemption or enforcement, will result in a loss on one or more Classes of Notes, beginning with the most junior
Class then outstanding.

**The Reserve Fund is the only source of liquidity in the transaction, is of a fixed size and is not available to
pay principal.**

The Issuer has not entered into a liquidity facility and no person has agreed to advance funds to the Issuer to
cover temporary shortfalls in collections. The only liquidity support is the Reserve Fund, which is funded by the
Originator on the Closing Date at the required amount set out in section 16. If the Available Funds on any Payment
Date are insufficient to pay the senior fees and interest on each Class in full, the shortfall is drawn from the
Reserve Fund, as described in section 15. The Reserve Fund is never used to pay principal on the Notes or the
deferred consideration payable to the Originator (the "Deferred Consideration").

The required amount of the Reserve Fund is fixed for the life of the transaction and does not increase if the
performance of the portfolio deteriorates. Once drawn, the Reserve Fund is replenished only from Available Funds
remaining after the payment of interest on all Classes, so a period of sustained shortfalls may exhaust it. A
disruption in collections affecting several Collection Periods, such as a failure of the Servicer (see section
2.4), a large-scale payment holiday imposed by law or regulation, or a market-wide disruption of payment systems,
could exceed the amount available in the Reserve Fund. In that case the Issuer may be unable to pay interest in
full on one or more Classes on the relevant Payment Date.

Because the Reserve Fund is sized by reference to the initial balance of the Notes and is not reduced as the Notes
amortise, it will become larger in proportion to the balance of the Notes over time. Noteholders should not,
however, regard the Reserve Fund as a source of principal protection: amounts standing to its credit are released
only in the circumstances set out in section 16, and a draw on the Reserve Fund is itself one of the tests whose
failure constitutes a Trigger Event.

**If Available Funds and the Reserve Fund are insufficient, the Issuer may be unable to pay interest on the junior
Classes when due.**

On each Payment Date, interest on a Class is paid only to the extent that Available Funds and amounts drawn from
the Reserve Fund remain after the payment of all items ranking above it in section 15. The Conditions in section 5
set out the consequences of an interest shortfall, including, for each Class other than the most senior Class then
outstanding (the "Most Senior Class"), the deferral of the unpaid amount to the next Payment Date, when it becomes
payable together with the interest then due. A deferral of interest on a junior Class does not, under the
Conditions, constitute an event of default while a more senior Class remains outstanding, and holders of a junior
Class will not be able to accelerate their Notes by reason of such a deferral.

A failure to pay interest in full on the Most Senior Class when due, however, may constitute an event of default
under the Conditions, entitling the Trustee, subject to the terms of the Trust Deed, to accelerate the Notes and
enforce the security. Following enforcement, the proceeds of the Charged Property are applied in the
post-enforcement order set out in the Conditions and the Deed of Charge, and there can be no assurance that those
proceeds will be sufficient to pay all amounts due to all Classes. Holders of junior Classes may therefore receive
no interest for extended periods and may suffer a loss of deferred interest as well as of principal.

**The loans pay fixed rates of interest while the Notes pay SONIA plus a margin, and this basis risk is not
hedged.**

Each loan bears a fixed rate of interest for its whole term, set at origination, while each Class of Notes bears
interest at SONIA plus the margin set out in section 4. The Issuer has not entered into an interest rate swap, a
cap or any other hedging arrangement, and it is not required to do so under the Transaction Documents. The
difference between the fixed yield on the portfolio and the floating rate payable on the Notes is therefore
borne entirely by the transaction. If SONIA rises, the interest payable on the Notes rises with it, while the
interest received on the loans does not change. The excess spread, being the difference between the interest
collected on the loans and the senior fees and interest payable on the Notes, will fall and, in a sufficiently
large rise in SONIA, may disappear entirely.

The principal mitigants of this basis risk are the excess spread available at the Closing Date, which reflects
the difference between the weighted average interest rate of the portfolio described in section 10 and the
margins on the Notes, and the Reserve Fund. Neither of them is sized to withstand every possible path of SONIA.
A reduction in excess spread reduces the amount available to cure any Principal Deficiency arising from defaults
and to replenish the Reserve Fund. A rise in SONIA combined with an increase in defaults, which may occur together
if higher interest rates in the wider economy put pressure on household finances (see section 2.5), would have a
compounded effect on the ability of the Issuer to pay interest and principal in full.

The effect of the basis risk is mitigated over time by the relatively short remaining term of the loans and by
the amortisation of the Notes, but it is not eliminated. Investors should form their own view of the future path
of SONIA and of the sensitivity of each Class to it, taking into account the weighted average life scenarios in
section 17, which do not model changes in SONIA.

**Prepayments of higher-yielding loans and defaults may reduce the excess spread over time.**

The excess spread of the portfolio is not constant. Loans which carry higher interest rates are typically made to
Borrowers with weaker credit profiles, who are more likely both to default and, where their circumstances
improve, to refinance. If loans with higher interest rates prepay or default at a faster rate than loans with lower
interest rates, the weighted average interest rate of the remaining portfolio will fall, and the excess spread
available to absorb losses and cure any Principal Deficiency will decrease. In addition, the senior fees include
fixed amounts, such as the Trustee fee described in section 15, which do not decrease as the Pool Balance
amortises and therefore absorb a growing share of the interest collections towards the end of the transaction.

**The determination of SONIA, and any change to or discontinuation of SONIA, may affect the amount of interest
payable on the Notes.**

The rate of interest on each Class for each interest period is determined by the Agent Bank in accordance with the
Conditions by reference to the Sterling Overnight Index Average ("SONIA") administered by the Bank of England. The
manner of determination set out in the Conditions, which uses SONIA observed over a period ending before the
end of the interest period, means that the amount of interest payable on each Payment Date will be known only
shortly before that date. Investors may find it difficult to forecast the interest which they will receive, and
their own systems must be able to process a rate determined in that way. SONIA is an overnight rate and may be more
volatile than term rates.

The Conditions contain fallback provisions which apply if SONIA is not published, or if the Bank of England or a
successor administrator ceases to publish it or announces a material change to its methodology. The fallbacks
include the use of the Bank Rate adjusted by a spread and, if those fallbacks fail, the rate determined for the
last period for which SONIA was available. The operation of the fallbacks may result in a rate which differs from
that which would have applied if SONIA had continued to be published, and, in the last resort, the Notes may in
effect become fixed rate notes. In addition, the Conditions permit the Trustee to agree, without the consent of
Noteholders, to modifications proposed by the Issuer or the Cash Manager to change the base rate of the Notes in
the circumstances set out in the Conditions, including where SONIA is replaced as the market standard for sterling
floating rate notes. Any such change may adversely affect the value of the Notes.

**The ratings of the Notes may not reflect all risks, may be lowered or withdrawn, and are not a recommendation to
buy, sell or hold the Notes.**

Each Class of Notes is expected on issue to be assigned the rating set out in section 4 by the Rating Agencies. A
rating addresses only the likelihood of timely payment of interest on the Most Senior Class and the ultimate
payment of principal on or before the Final Maturity Date, and, for the other Classes, the matters set out in the
relevant Rating Agency's published methodology. A rating does not address the likelihood or timing of prepayments,
the possibility that the Notes will be redeemed earlier than expected, the payment of deferred interest except as
stated in the relevant methodology, the market value or liquidity of the Notes, or the suitability of the Notes for
any investor. A rating is not a recommendation to buy, sell or hold securities and may be revised, suspended or
withdrawn at any time by the assigning Rating Agency.

The ratings are based on the information provided by the Originator and the Arranger about the portfolio, the
Originator's historical performance data and the structure of the transaction, and on the Rating Agencies' current
criteria. Those criteria may change, and a change in criteria may lead to a downgrade of one or more Classes even if
the portfolio performs as expected. A downgrade of a counterparty, in particular the Account Bank, may also
lead to a downgrade of the Notes if the counterparty is not replaced in accordance with the Transaction Documents.
A downgrade of the United Kingdom's sovereign rating may limit the maximum rating which the Rating Agencies assign to
UK structured finance transactions.

Rating agencies other than the Rating Agencies may assign unsolicited ratings to the Notes. An unsolicited rating
may be lower than the rating assigned by the Rating Agencies and could adversely affect the market value and
liquidity of the Notes. Under the UK credit rating agencies regulation, certain regulated investors may use a
rating for regulatory purposes only if it is issued or endorsed by a credit rating agency registered or certified in
the United Kingdom. Investors should consult their own advisers on the regulatory treatment of the ratings.

**The yield to maturity and the weighted average life of each Class are uncertain and depend on the rate of
prepayment, default and voluntary termination of the loans.**

The rate at which principal is repaid on the Notes depends on the rate at which the Pool Balance falls, which
depends in turn on scheduled amortisation, on the payment of Balloon Payments (as defined in section 2.2) at the end of PCP Agreements, on
voluntary prepayments and early settlements, on voluntary terminations under the Consumer Credit Act 1974 (the
"CCA"), on defaults and on repurchases of loans by the Seller under section 12. None of these can be predicted with
certainty. The rate of prepayment of motor finance loans is influenced by a wide range of factors, including the
availability and cost of alternative finance, the value of the Vehicle relative to the outstanding balance, new
vehicle launches and manufacturer incentives, the desire of Borrowers to change their Vehicles, insurance write-offs,
and changes in the personal circumstances of Borrowers.

The weighted average lives shown in section 17 are calculated on the basis of stated assumptions, including a
constant rate of prepayment, the absence of defaults and the exercise or non-exercise of the Clean-Up Call (as
defined below). The actual performance of the portfolio will differ from those assumptions, and the actual
weighted average life of each Class may be materially shorter or longer than the figures shown. An investor who
purchases Notes at a premium to their principal amount faces the risk that faster than expected repayment will
reduce its yield; an investor who purchases at a discount faces the risk that slower than expected repayment will
reduce its yield. A switch to sequential payment following a Trigger Event will change the weighted average lives
of all Classes, as described above.

The loans in the portfolio have a concentration of scheduled cash flows at the end of the PCP Agreements, when the
Balloon Payment falls due and the Borrower chooses whether to pay it, to refinance it or to return the Vehicle.
The timing of principal receipts on the Notes will therefore be sensitive to the timing of those maturities and to
the choices made by Borrowers, as described in section 2.2.

**The Notes may be redeemed early at the option of the Issuer, and Noteholders may be unable to reinvest the
proceeds at the same yield.**

The Issuer may redeem all, but not some only, of the Notes at their principal amount outstanding, together with
accrued interest, on any Payment Date on which the Pool Balance is less than 10% of the Pool Balance at the
cut-off date (the "Clean-Up Call"), provided that it has sufficient funds to do so, in accordance with the
Conditions. The Issuer expects to fund the exercise of the Clean-Up Call from the proceeds of a repurchase of the
remaining loans by the Seller, and the Seller has no obligation to make that repurchase. The Conditions also
permit the Issuer to redeem the Notes in whole if certain changes in tax law occur, as described in section 5.

An early redemption will shorten the life of the Notes. Noteholders may not be able to reinvest the redemption
proceeds at an effective yield as high as that of the Notes, particularly at a time of falling interest rates. The
possibility of an optional redemption may also limit the market value of the Notes, which is unlikely to rise
substantially above the price at which they may be redeemed while the Issuer may exercise the option. Conversely,
there can be no assurance that the Clean-Up Call will be exercised, and Noteholders should not base their
investment on the assumption that the Notes will be redeemed on the first Payment Date on which it becomes
exercisable.

**The Notes may not be repaid in full by the Final Maturity Date, and the tail of the portfolio may extend
their repayment.**

Each Class of Notes, if not previously redeemed, is due to be redeemed in full on
[[final_maturity_date]] (the "Final Maturity Date"). The Final Maturity Date has been set at a date after the
latest scheduled maturity of any loan in the portfolio to allow time for the collection of late payments and of
recoveries on defaulted loans. There can be no assurance, however, that the Charged Property will produce enough
cash by that date to redeem all Classes in full. Loans may be rescheduled under forbearance arrangements so that
they mature later than originally scheduled, recoveries may take longer than expected, particularly where a court
order is required to recover a Vehicle protected under section 90 of the CCA, and the proceeds of litigation
against Borrowers may be received only after several years.

If on the Final Maturity Date the amounts available to the Issuer are insufficient to redeem a Class in full, the
unpaid balance will remain due, but, because the Notes are limited recourse, Noteholders will have no further claim
once the Charged Property has been realised and distributed. Holders of junior Classes bear this risk first.
Noteholders should also note that the Issuer is not obliged to redeem any Class before the Final Maturity Date
other than from the Available Funds in accordance with section 15, and that the expected maturity of each Class
shown in section 17 is an estimate only.

**There is no established secondary market for the Notes, the Notes are not listed, and Noteholders may be
unable to sell them or may have to sell at a substantial discount.**

The Notes are not, and will not be, admitted to listing or trading on any stock exchange or market, and they are
offered only to professional investors in the United Kingdom and the European Economic Area, as described in
section 20. There is currently no secondary market for the Notes and there can be no assurance that one will
develop or, if one develops, that it will provide Noteholders with liquidity or will continue for the life of the
Notes. The Lead Manager has no obligation to make a market in the Notes. The absence of a listing may limit the
categories of investors which are permitted to hold the Notes and may affect their eligibility as collateral in
central bank or market operations.

The market for asset-backed securities has in the past experienced periods of severe disruption, during which
prices fell sharply and trading volumes contracted, without any change in the performance of the underlying
assets. A recurrence of such conditions, or a reduction in the number of investors in UK motor finance
securitisations, for example as a result of the regulatory and litigation developments described in section 2.3,
could make the Notes difficult to sell. Noteholders should be prepared to hold their Notes until their final
redemption.

The Notes are issued in minimum denominations of £100,000 and integral multiples of £1,000 above that amount. A
Noteholder who holds an amount which is not an integral multiple of the minimum denomination may, as a result of
trading, hold an amount less than £100,000 and in that case may be unable to transfer that holding and, if
definitive notes are issued, may not receive a definitive note in respect of it.

**Because the Notes are held in global registered form through the clearing systems, Noteholders will depend on
those systems for transfers, payments and communications.**

Each Class of Notes is represented on issue by a global note in registered form, held by or on behalf of a common
depositary or nominee for the clearing systems specified in section 21. Definitive notes will be issued only in the
limited circumstances set out in the global notes. While the Notes are held in global form, investors will be able
to trade their beneficial interests only through the clearing systems and their participants, and the Issuer will
discharge its payment obligations by making payments to or to the order of the registered holder of the global
note. Investors must rely on the procedures of the clearing systems and their participants to receive payments,
notices and voting materials, and to exercise their rights. Neither the Issuer nor the Trustee has any
responsibility for the records of the clearing systems or for any failure by them to pass on payments or notices.

**The Trustee will have regard to the interests of the Noteholders as a class, will in a conflict give priority to
the Most Senior Class, and is not obliged to act unless indemnified to its satisfaction.**

The Trust Deed and the Deed of Charge provide that, in the exercise of its powers, trusts, authorities and
discretions, the Trustee will have regard to the interests of the Noteholders of each Class as a class and will not
have regard to the consequences of such exercise for individual Noteholders, including any tax consequences
arising from their residence or domicile. Where, in the opinion of the Trustee, there is a conflict between the
interests of the holders of different Classes, the Trustee will have regard only to the interests of the holders of
the Most Senior Class then outstanding. The interests of the holders of a junior Class may therefore be prejudiced
by a decision of the Trustee which favours the holders of a more senior Class, for example in relation to the timing
or manner of enforcement of the security.

The Trustee is not obliged to take any action, including the enforcement of the security, unless it has been
directed to do so by an extraordinary resolution of the holders of the Most Senior Class or requested in writing by
the holders of the required percentage of the principal amount outstanding of the Most Senior Class, and has been
indemnified, secured or prefunded to its satisfaction. The Trustee's fees, costs and indemnities rank ahead of
payments on the Notes. A delay in obtaining directions or indemnities may delay enforcement and reduce the amount
ultimately recovered.

**The Trustee may agree to modifications of the Transaction Documents and the Conditions without the consent of
Noteholders, and resolutions passed by the required majority bind all Noteholders.**

The Conditions and the Trust Deed contain provisions for convening meetings of Noteholders, and for written
resolutions, to consider matters affecting their interests. These provisions permit defined majorities to bind all
Noteholders, including Noteholders who did not attend or vote at the relevant meeting and Noteholders who voted in a
manner contrary to the majority. Certain fundamental matters, such as a change to the amount, date or currency of
any payment on the Notes or to the priority of payments, require a resolution passed by a higher majority of each
affected Class. Other matters may be determined by a resolution of the Most Senior Class alone.

The Trustee may also agree, without the consent of Noteholders, to any modification of the Conditions or the
Transaction Documents which in its opinion is not materially prejudicial to the interests of the holders of the
Most Senior Class, or which is of a formal, minor or technical nature or is made to correct a manifest error. In
addition, the Conditions require the Trustee to agree, without the consent of Noteholders, to modifications which
the Issuer certifies are required to comply with changes in the UK securitisation rules, to implement the criteria
of the Rating Agencies relating to counterparties, to replace the base rate of the Notes in the circumstances
described above, or to comply with tax reporting requirements, subject to the right of holders of a specified
percentage of the Most Senior Class to object within the period stated in the Conditions. Noteholders may therefore
find that their rights have changed without their consent, and such changes may adversely affect the value of
their Notes.

**The transaction parties perform several roles in the transaction and may have interests which conflict with
those of the Noteholders.**

Kestrel Motor Finance Ltd acts as originator, seller and servicer, holds the Notes required to be retained under
the vertical slice described in section 18, funds the Reserve Fund and receives the Deferred Consideration. As
servicer it makes decisions on arrears management, forbearance, the timing and manner of Vehicle recoveries and the
handling of complaints and redress which affect the collections of the Issuer, and those decisions may also affect
its own commercial interests, including its relationships with Borrowers, motor dealers and vehicle manufacturers
and the performance of the loans which it retains on its own balance sheet. The servicing agreement between the Issuer, the Servicer and the Trustee (the "Servicing Agreement") requires the
Servicer to service the loans with the same degree of skill and care which it applies to loans held for its own
account, but there can be no assurance that a conflict will not arise.

**The Issuer may become subject to insolvency or restructuring proceedings, and the security may be affected by

The Issuer has granted fixed security over the loans, its rights under the Transaction Documents and its accounts,

preferential creditors (which since 2020 include certain amounts owed to HM Revenue & Customs as secondary
preferential debts) and the prescribed part reserved for unsecured creditors, before the Trustee could apply them
for the Noteholders. The Issuer, as a special purpose vehicle with no employees and with obligations to HMRC under

The Issuer is party to a capital market arrangement within the meaning of the Insolvency Act 1986. On that basis,
the Trustee is entitled to appoint an administrative receiver of the Issuer under the capital market exception to the

## 2.2 Risks relating to the loans

**Borrowers may fail to pay the amounts due under the Loan Agreements, and the Issuer bears the credit risk of
the Borrowers.**

The ability of each Borrower to make the payments due under its Loan Agreement depends on the Borrower's income,
employment, other financial commitments and personal circumstances, any of which may change over the life of the
loan. Events such as unemployment, a reduction in working hours or earnings, illness, disability, death,
relationship breakdown, an increase in housing or energy costs, or the accumulation of other debt may reduce a
Borrower's ability or willingness to pay. Motor finance is generally regarded by consumers as a priority debt
because the Vehicle is often needed for work, but a Borrower in financial difficulty may nevertheless fall into
arrears and default. When a loan defaults, as defined in section 11, it leaves the Pool Balance and the Issuer
depends on recoveries, mainly from the sale of the Vehicle, to recover part of its defaulted balance.

The Issuer bears the full credit risk of the Borrowers. No credit insurance, guarantee or other credit support has
been obtained by the Issuer in respect of any loan. The rate of default on the loans
may be higher than the rates experienced by the Originator historically or assumed by the Rating Agencies, and
higher defaults will reduce the amounts available to pay the Notes, as described in section 2.1.

**The credit scores and underwriting criteria used by the Originator may not predict the performance of the
loans.**

The Originator underwrote each loan in accordance with its lending policy at the time of origination, using a
combination of credit reference agency data, its own application scorecards, affordability assessments and
fraud checks, as described in section 9. The credit scores shown in section 10 are those recorded on the
Originator's systems at origination or at the latest update and reflect the scoring model in use at that time; they
are not a uniform measure and are not comparable with scores calculated by other lenders or credit reference
agencies. A credit score is a statistical estimate of the likelihood of default based on past behaviour and does
not take into account events which have occurred since the score was calculated.

The Originator's lending policy has changed over time and may continue to change, and the loans in the portfolio
were originated under different versions of it. The affordability rules in the FCA's Consumer Credit sourcebook
("CONC") require a lender to assess the creditworthiness of the customer and the affordability of the credit, but an
assessment made at origination relies on information supplied by the Borrower and by third parties which may be
incomplete, inaccurate or, in the case of fraud, false. There can be no assurance that the underwriting process
identified all Borrowers who would be unable to meet their obligations.

**The portfolio is concentrated in particular regions of the United Kingdom, and a regional downturn could
increase losses.**

The geographical distribution of the Borrowers at the cut-off date, by region of residence, is shown in the regional distribution table
in section 10. Certain regions represent a larger share of the Pool Balance than others. A deterioration in economic
conditions in a region in which there is a concentration of Borrowers, such as the closure of a major employer, a
regional rise in unemployment, or a local natural event such as flooding, may have a disproportionate effect on the
performance of the loans. The regions also differ in their levels of household income and indebtedness, in the
density and condition of their used car markets, which affect the speed and price of Vehicle sales, and in local
transport policies, such as clean air and low emission zones, which may affect the value and use of certain
Vehicles (see below).

The portfolio includes loans to Borrowers resident in Scotland and in Northern Ireland. The CCA applies
throughout the United Kingdom, but the law relating to the transfer of the loans, the recovery of debts and the
enforcement of court orders differs in those jurisdictions from the law of England and Wales. The procedures for
recovering Vehicles and enforcing judgments in those jurisdictions may take longer or cost more than in England and
Wales, and the transfer of the Scottish loans to the Issuer is made by a different legal mechanism, as described in
section 2.3.

**The loans were originated through a network of motor dealers, and problems affecting a dealer, a dealer group
or a vehicle manufacturer could affect a number of loans together.**

The Originator originates loans through motor dealers acting as credit brokers, which introduce customers who wish
to finance the purchase of a vehicle from the dealer, and the portfolio may include loans introduced by a limited
number of large dealer groups. Concentrations of this kind expose the Issuer to the conduct and solvency of those
dealers. Misconduct by a dealer at the point of sale, such as mis-selling, failure to disclose commission, fraud or
misdescription of a Vehicle, may give rise to claims by the Borrowers concerned against the Originator as creditor
which, as described in section 2.3, may be exercisable against the Issuer by way of set-off or under section 56 or
section 140A of the CCA. The insolvency of a dealer may also cause Vehicle warranties and service plans sold
alongside the finance to fail, which may lead Borrowers to dispute or delay payments.

The portfolio also includes concentrations of Vehicles of particular manufacturers, models and fuel types. A
product recall, the discovery of a design defect, the withdrawal of a manufacturer or brand from the United Kingdom,
the failure of a manufacturer's dealer network, or a sharp reduction in the new vehicle prices of a manufacturer may
reduce the value of the Vehicles of that manufacturer and therefore the recoveries and the residual value proceeds
received by the Issuer. Where the Originator has arrangements with a manufacturer, for example for subsidised
interest rates, the termination of those arrangements may also affect the Originator's business and its ability to
continue as Servicer.

**PCP Agreements require a Balloon Payment at the end of the term, and Borrowers may return the Vehicle instead of
paying it, exposing the Issuer to the residual value of the Vehicle.**

Under a PCP Agreement, the monthly instalments repay only part of the amount financed and the final payment due at
the end of the term (the "Balloon Payment", also called the optional final payment) is set at origination by
reference to the Originator's estimate of the future value of the Vehicle at the end of the agreement, taking into
account its expected age and the annual mileage agreed with the Borrower. The aggregate amount of the Balloon
Payments in the portfolio at the cut-off date is shown in section 10. At the end of the term the Borrower may pay the
Balloon Payment and acquire title to the Vehicle, refinance the Balloon Payment, often with a new PCP Agreement on
another vehicle through the same dealer, or return the Vehicle in satisfaction of the Balloon Payment, subject to
charges for excess mileage and for damage beyond fair wear and tear.

A Borrower has an economic incentive to return the Vehicle when its market value is lower than the Balloon Payment.
If the market value of used vehicles at the end of the PCP Agreements is lower than the values assumed at
origination, a larger proportion of Borrowers will return their Vehicles, and the proceeds of sale of those Vehicles
will be lower than the Balloon Payments which they replace. The shortfall between the Balloon Payment and the net
sale proceeds of a returned Vehicle (a "Residual Value Loss") is borne by the Issuer. The Originator gives no
guarantee of the residual value of any Vehicle to the Issuer, and Residual Value Losses are not covered by any
insurance or by any manufacturer guarantee in favour of the Issuer.

Residual Value Losses are concentrated in time, because they arise at the maturity of the PCP Agreements, and they
are correlated, because a fall in used vehicle prices affects all Vehicles of a given type at the same time. A
large number of PCP Agreements maturing in a period of weak used vehicle prices may therefore produce substantial
losses within a short time. Because returned Vehicles are not defaults within the meaning of section 11, Residual
Value Losses may not be reflected in the cumulative default ratio used in the trigger tests in section 16, although
they reduce the Available Funds and may give rise to a Principal Deficiency. The Originator's residual value
setting policy, and the risk that its estimates were too high for particular models or fuel types, are discussed in
section 9 and in the risk factor on electric vehicles below.

**Borrowers have a statutory right to terminate their agreements voluntarily under sections 99 and 100 of the
CCA, which may leave the Issuer with a loss on the Vehicle.**

Section 99 of the CCA gives the debtor under a regulated hire purchase or conditional sale agreement the right to
terminate the agreement at any time before the final payment falls due, by giving notice to the creditor, and to
return the goods. Both the HP Agreements and the PCP Agreements are regulated hire purchase agreements to which
this right applies. Under section 100 of the CCA, the Borrower's liability on voluntary termination is limited, in
general, to the amount needed to bring the total payments made to one-half of the total price payable under the
agreement, together with any arrears and compensation for any failure to take reasonable care of the Vehicle. A
court may order a smaller payment if it is satisfied that the creditor's loss is less. Once a Borrower has paid
one-half of the total price, the Borrower may return the Vehicle with no further liability, other than for damage.

For PCP Agreements, the total price includes the Balloon Payment, so that the half-way point is reached later in the
term than under an HP Agreement of the same amount, but the outstanding balance at that point is still large,
because the Balloon Payment has not been paid. Where the value of the Vehicle is lower than the outstanding balance
at the time of voluntary termination, the Issuer will suffer a loss equal to the difference between the balance and
the net proceeds of sale of the returned Vehicle, plus any payment which the Borrower is obliged to make under
section 100. Voluntary termination rights are exercised more frequently when used vehicle prices fall, when
Borrowers face financial difficulty or when Borrowers wish to change their Vehicle, and they have received wide
publicity, in part through claims management companies and consumer media. The risk is greatest for loans with a
high loan-to-value ratio at origination and for Vehicles whose values have depreciated faster than expected.

**The amount recovered from the sale of Vehicles after default or return may be lower than expected, and
recoveries may be delayed.**

When a loan defaults, the Servicer may terminate the Loan Agreement and repossess the Vehicle, sell it, usually
through an auction or online remarketing channel, and pursue the Borrower for any shortfall, in accordance with its
collection policies described in section 13. The amount recovered depends on the condition, age and mileage of the
Vehicle at the time of recovery, the state of the used car market, the costs of recovery, storage, preparation and
sale, and the ability of the Borrower to pay any shortfall. Vehicles recovered after a default are frequently in
worse condition than average and may have been damaged, stripped of equipment, untaxed or uninsured. Some Vehicles
cannot be located, have been sold to third parties, exported or written off, and the Issuer may recover nothing in
respect of them other than any amounts paid by the Borrower or an insurer. The recovery assumption stated in section
11 is an expectation of the Servicer and is not guaranteed.

The repossession of a Vehicle is subject to the restrictions of the CCA. Under section 87 the creditor must serve a
default notice and give the Borrower at least 14 days to remedy the breach before terminating the agreement or
recovering the goods. Under section 90, once the Borrower has paid at least one-third of the total price, the
Vehicle becomes "protected goods" and the creditor may not recover possession without a court order unless the
Borrower consents or terminates the agreement. Under section 92, the creditor may not enter premises to take
possession of the Vehicle without a court order. A creditor who recovers protected goods in breach of section 90
loses its right to any further payment and must repay the sums paid. The need to obtain court orders, and any
time orders granted by the court under section 129 of the CCA, may delay recoveries significantly and increase their
costs.

The FCA's rules and guidance on the treatment of customers in arrears or default, including those in CONC 7 and the
Consumer Duty, require the Servicer to treat Borrowers in financial difficulty with forbearance and due
consideration, to consider alternatives to repossession, and to treat repossession as a last resort. Compliance with
these requirements may delay or reduce recoveries. Delays in recoveries mean that defaulted balances remain unpaid
for longer, increasing the time during which a Principal Deficiency may be outstanding.

**The value of electric and hybrid Vehicles, and of petrol and diesel Vehicles, is exposed to the transition away
from internal combustion engines.**

The portfolio includes loans secured on battery electric Vehicles, hybrid Vehicles, petrol Vehicles and diesel
Vehicles in the proportions shown in the fuel type distribution table in section 10. The United Kingdom has legislated for a transition to
zero emission vehicles. The zero emission vehicle mandate requires manufacturers to ensure that a rising proportion
of their new car sales in Great Britain are zero emission, with the sale of new cars powered solely by petrol or
diesel to end in 2030 and the sale of new hybrid cars to end in 2035. These policies, together with changes in
vehicle excise duty, in company car taxation, in the availability of grants for electric vehicles, and proposals for
distance-based charging of electric vehicles, will affect the relative demand for, and value of, Vehicles of
different fuel types over the life of the loans. Their timing and content have changed more than once and may change
again.

Used battery electric vehicles have experienced in recent years faster and more volatile depreciation than
comparable petrol vehicles, as a result of rapid price reductions on new electric vehicles by manufacturers,
discounting required to meet the zero emission vehicle mandate, technological improvement which makes older models
less attractive, uncertainty about battery health and replacement cost, and the limited depth of the used electric
vehicle market. Residual values set at origination for electric Vehicles may therefore prove too high, increasing
the likelihood of hand-backs and Residual Value Losses at the end of PCP Agreements and the losses on voluntary
terminations and recoveries. The Originator's valuation of electric Vehicles depends on the availability of reliable
battery health data, which is not yet standard in the used car market.

Diesel and older petrol Vehicles face different risks. Clean air zones, the ultra low emission zone in London and
similar schemes in other cities impose daily charges on vehicles which do not meet the relevant emission standards,
and such schemes may be extended or tightened. Demand for these Vehicles in the used market may fall as
the transition progresses and as the supply of charging infrastructure increases. Conversely, if the transition
slows, used electric vehicle values may fall further relative to other fuel types. The combined effect of these
factors on the portfolio cannot be predicted.

**Borrowers may settle their loans early, and the rate of prepayment cannot be predicted.**

A Borrower under a regulated agreement has the statutory right under section 94 of the CCA to discharge its
indebtedness early, in whole or in part, at any time, by notice to the creditor and payment of the amount due. On
early settlement, the Borrower is entitled to a rebate of charges for credit calculated in accordance with the
Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004, and the creditor may in certain circumstances claim
compensation for the cost of early repayment only within the limits set by section 95A of the CCA. Borrowers commonly
settle their loans early when they part-exchange their Vehicle for another vehicle, when they sell their Vehicle
privately, or when their Vehicle is written off and the insurance proceeds are applied to the settlement amount.

Early settlements are passed to the Issuer as principal collections and accelerate the repayment of the Notes, as
described in section 2.1. A higher rate of prepayment will shorten the weighted average lives of the Notes and
reduce the total interest received by the Issuer over the life of the transaction, and therefore the total excess
spread available. The rate of prepayment is affected by the behaviour of motor dealers, which may encourage
Borrowers to change their Vehicles before the end of their agreements, by manufacturers' sales campaigns, by
interest rates and by the redress and litigation developments described in section 2.3, which may cause Borrowers to
settle or refinance their agreements.

**Borrowers may be entitled to set off claims against the Originator against amounts due under their loans, and
the Issuer would bear the resulting reduction in collections.**

The sale of the loans to the Issuer takes effect as an equitable assignment, and Borrowers will not be notified of
it unless a perfection event occurs, as described in section 2.3. Until notice of the assignment is given to a
Borrower, the Borrower may set off against the amounts due under its Loan Agreement any claim which it has against
the Originator, whether or not connected with the Loan Agreement. After notice is given, the Borrower may continue to
set off claims which arise out of the Loan Agreement itself or are closely connected with it, and claims against the
Originator which accrued before the notice. Claims of this kind include claims for damages for breach of the implied
terms as to the quality and fitness of the Vehicle, claims under section 56 of the CCA, under which the dealer is treated as the agent of the creditor in the
negotiations before the agreement, claims arising from an
unfair relationship under section 140A, and claims for redress for undisclosed or unfair commission (see section 2.3).

The exercise of a right of set-off reduces the amount which the Borrower pays and therefore the collections of the
Issuer. The Seller has agreed in the Transaction Documents to pay to the Issuer an amount equal to any reduction in the
amount received from a Borrower as a result of the exercise of a right of set-off against the Seller, or of a
reduction in the balance of a loan made by the Seller in settlement of a complaint or redress claim (a "Deemed
Collection"). The Issuer's protection against set-off risk therefore depends on the creditworthiness of the Seller,
and if the Seller becomes insolvent the Issuer will have only an unsecured claim against it for any Deemed Collection
which it has failed to pay.

**The proportion of new and used Vehicles in the portfolio affects its credit and residual value
performance.**

The portfolio includes loans for the purchase of new Vehicles and of used Vehicles in the proportions shown in
the vehicle type distribution table in section 10. Loans for new Vehicles are often subsidised by manufacturers, are typically larger and
have a higher proportion of PCP Agreements with larger Balloon Payments, and their Vehicles depreciate most rapidly in
the first years after registration. Loans for used Vehicles are typically smaller, but have historically been made
to Borrowers with a weaker credit profile, carry higher interest rates and have shown higher rates of default.
Used Vehicles are more likely to need repairs, which may lead to disputes about their quality, and their values are
more dispersed and harder to estimate. A change in the mix of the portfolio as loans amortise, prepay or default may
change its credit and residual value profile, and the performance of either segment may diverge from historical
experience.

**Loans with high loan-to-value ratios expose the Issuer to a larger loss if the Borrower defaults early in the
life of the loan.**

The original loan-to-value ratio of each loan, being the amount financed divided by the value of the Vehicle at
origination, is shown in the original loan-to-value distribution table in section 10, and the eligibility criteria in section 11 set a maximum ratio. A
vehicle typically loses a substantial part of its value in its first years, and the outstanding balance of a loan
with a high loan-to-value ratio, particularly a PCP Agreement, may remain above the value of the Vehicle for much of
its term. A Borrower who has little or no equity in the Vehicle has less incentive to continue paying in financial
difficulty, more incentive to exercise the right of voluntary termination, and, if the loan defaults, the Issuer's
loss will be larger. The amount financed may also include the cost of insurance products, service plans or other
add-ons sold with the Vehicle, which have no resale value. The eligibility criteria include requirements as to
seasoning and remaining instalments, but loans with relatively short seasoning carry a higher risk of early default
than mature loans.

**The Issuer may lose its title to a Vehicle if a Borrower sells it to a private purchaser in breach of the Loan
Agreement.**

Under an HP Agreement or a PCP Agreement, the creditor owns the Vehicle until the Borrower has paid all amounts due and
exercised the option to purchase. The Seller has assigned to the Issuer, or holds on trust for it, its rights in
relation to the Vehicles, as described in section 12. Under Part III of the Hire-Purchase Act 1964, however, a private
purchaser who buys a motor vehicle in good faith and without notice of the hire purchase agreement from a debtor
under that agreement acquires good title to the vehicle. If a Borrower sells a Vehicle in breach of its agreement, the
Issuer may lose its ability to recover the Vehicle and will have only a claim against the Borrower. The Originator
registers its interest in each Vehicle with the industry vehicle finance register, which allows trade purchasers to
check whether a vehicle is subject to finance, but registration does not prevent sales to private purchasers. Vehicle
fraud, including cloning, false identity applications and the export of financed vehicles, may also lead to losses
for which the Issuer has no recourse other than to the Seller under the loan warranties.

**Forbearance, payment arrangements and payment holidays may reduce or delay collections.**

The Servicer offers forbearance to Borrowers in financial difficulty in accordance with its collection policies
described in section 13 and with the FCA's rules, which require a creditor to consider the individual circumstances
of the customer and the range of forbearance options available, including reductions in or suspension of payments,
the waiver of interest or charges, and the extension of the term. Forbearance may reduce the interest and principal
received by the Issuer, extend the life of the loans beyond their scheduled maturity and delay the identification of
loans which will ultimately default. Loans subject to forbearance may continue to be reported as performing for the
purposes of the trigger tests in section 16 while their collections are reduced.

During the coronavirus pandemic, the FCA required consumer credit firms, including motor finance lenders, to offer
payment deferrals to customers affected by the pandemic without assessing their creditworthiness and to suspend
repossessions for a period. Similar measures could be imposed in response to a future pandemic, natural disaster,
economic shock or other emergency, and could affect a large number of loans at once, reducing collections over
several Collection Periods to an extent which the Reserve Fund may not cover.

**The Issuer relies on the loan warranties given by the Seller and on the Seller's ability to repurchase loans
which breach them.**

The Seller has given loan warranties to the Issuer as at the cut-off date and the Closing Date, which are described in
section 12, including warranties that each loan met the eligibility criteria in section 11, was originated in
compliance with the CCA and the applicable FCA rules, and is enforceable. The Issuer, the Trustee and the Arranger have
not carried out, and will not carry out, any investigation into whether the warranties are true. If a warranty is
breached and the breach is not remedied, the Seller is obliged to repurchase the loan or to indemnify the Issuer,
as described in section 12. This is the only remedy of the Issuer for a breach. The value of the remedy depends on the
continuing solvency of the Seller and on its ability to fund the repurchase price; no person guarantees the Seller's
obligations, and if the Seller is unable to pay, the Issuer will bear the loss.

The warranties are given only as at the dates stated and do not cover events which occur after those dates, such as a
change in law which renders a loan unenforceable, the later failure of the Servicer to comply with the CCA, or a
change in the regulatory treatment of commission. The warranties may also be qualified by reference to the Seller's
knowledge. Breaches which are identified only after the Seller's repurchase obligation has expired, or which arise
from matters not covered by the warranties, will not be remedied.

## 2.3 Legal and regulatory risks

**A Loan Agreement which does not comply with the CCA may be unenforceable, or enforceable only with the
permission of the court, and the Borrower may recover amounts paid.**

Each of the loans is a regulated credit agreement for the purposes of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000
("FSMA") and the CCA, and its form, content and execution are governed by the CCA, by the regulations made under it,
including the Consumer Credit (Agreements) Regulations 2010 and the Consumer Credit (Disclosure of Information)
Regulations 2010, and by the FCA's rules in CONC. Under section 61 of the CCA, an agreement is properly executed only if
it is in the prescribed form, contains all the prescribed terms and conforms to the regulations, is signed in the
prescribed manner by the debtor and by or on behalf of the creditor, and is in a state in which all its terms are
readily legible when presented for signature. Under section 65, an improperly executed agreement is enforceable
against the debtor only on an order of the court. Under section 127, the court must dismiss an application for an
enforcement order if it considers it just to do so, having regard to the prejudice caused by the contravention and
the degree of culpability for it, and may reduce or discharge any sum payable by the debtor to compensate it for
prejudice suffered.

The Originator uses standard form agreements and an electronic signature process for most loans, and has warranted
that each Loan Agreement complies with the CCA, as described in section 12. Nevertheless, technical requirements of
the CCA are detailed and have been the subject of extensive litigation, including on the calculation and disclosure
of the annual percentage rate and the total amount payable, the content of pre-contract information, the
description of the right of withdrawal under section 66A, and the treatment of charges for optional products.
Errors in the Originator's documentation or systems may affect a large number of loans at once. If a court were to
hold that a type of Loan Agreement used by the Originator is improperly executed, the loans concerned would be
unenforceable without a court order, Borrowers may cease paying, and claims management companies may encourage them
to do so. The Issuer would depend on the Seller's repurchase obligation and on its solvency to recover the resulting
loss.

In addition, a creditor who enters into a regulated credit agreement through an unauthorised credit broker, or which
is itself not authorised, may be unable to enforce it without the approval of the FCA under sections 26 to 28 of
FSMA. The Originator is authorised by the FCA, and the Seller has warranted that each loan was introduced by a broker
holding the required permission, but a breach of this requirement could make the affected loans unenforceable.

**A failure by the Servicer to comply with the CCA information requirements during the life of a loan may prevent
the Issuer from enforcing it and from charging interest.**

The CCA imposes continuing information requirements on the creditor under a regulated agreement. These include the
obligation under section 77A to provide the debtor with an annual statement in the prescribed form, the obligation
under section 86B to give notices of sums in arrears in the prescribed form when the arrears reach the specified
level, the obligation under section 86C to give notices of default sums, and the requirement under section 86E that
default interest may be charged only as simple interest. Under section 77A, a creditor who fails to give an annual
statement is unable to enforce the agreement during the period of non-compliance, and the debtor has no liability to
pay any interest or default sum for that period. Under section 86D, similar consequences apply to a failure to give
an arrears notice when required. A failure to give the information required by section 77, on the debtor's request,
also prevents enforcement while the failure continues.

These obligations are performed by the Servicer on behalf of the Issuer. A systems error affecting the content,
format or timing of statements or notices may affect a large number of loans and may not be identified for some
time, so that the loss of interest may relate to a long period. Although a breach of these requirements may often
be remedied by the issue of corrected statements, the interest attributable to the period of non-compliance is not
recoverable and may have to be refunded. The Servicer has agreed to indemnify the Issuer against losses arising
from its failure to comply with its obligations under the Servicing Agreement, but the value of that indemnity
depends on the Servicer's solvency.

**A court may find that the relationship between a Borrower and the creditor is unfair and may order the creditor
to repay amounts to the Borrower or reduce the amounts owed.**

Under sections 140A to 140C of the CCA, a court may determine that the relationship between a creditor and a debtor
arising out of a credit agreement is unfair to the debtor because of any of the terms of the agreement, the way in
which the creditor has exercised or enforced its rights, or any other thing done or not done by or on behalf of the
creditor, before or after the making of the agreement. Once the debtor alleges that the relationship is unfair, the
burden of proving the contrary lies on the creditor. If the court makes a determination of unfairness, it may make a
wide range of orders, including requiring the creditor, or any associate or former associate of the creditor, to
repay sums paid by the debtor, reducing or discharging any sum payable by the debtor, or altering the terms of the
agreement. The provisions apply to the relationship as it exists at the time of the determination and may be applied
to agreements which have been repaid in full.

The Issuer, as assignee, is the creditor under the Loan Agreements for the purposes of the CCA, and a court may make
an order under section 140B against the Issuer in respect of acts or omissions of the Originator or of a dealer
acting as credit broker before the sale of the loans. Matters which may give rise to an unfair relationship include
the non-disclosure of commission paid to the dealer, its nature or its amount, the existence of tied or exclusive
arrangements between the lender and the dealer, mis-selling of the finance or of add-on products, and unfair
collection practices. The Supreme Court in its judgment of August 2025 in the joined motor finance appeals held that
dealers acting as credit brokers did not owe the fiduciary duties which would have supported claims in bribery or
for breach of fiduciary duty, but confirmed that the non-disclosure of a commission arrangement can, depending on the
circumstances, render the relationship unfair under section 140A. The outcome of claims of this type depends on the
facts of each case. The Seller's obligation to pay Deemed Collections and to indemnify the Issuer, and its loan
warranties, are the Issuer's protection against this risk.

**Because the creditor under an HP Agreement or a PCP Agreement is also the supplier of the Vehicle, Borrowers may
bring claims relating to the quality of the Vehicle against the Issuer.**

Under a hire purchase agreement, the creditor is the owner and supplier of the goods. The terms implied by the
Consumer Rights Act 2015 into contracts for the hire purchase of goods with consumers, as to the satisfactory quality
and fitness for purpose of the goods and their conformity with their description, are therefore given by the
creditor, and a Borrower whose Vehicle is defective may claim against the creditor and, in appropriate cases,
reject the Vehicle, claim a price reduction or require repair or replacement. The rights of a consumer under the
Consumer Rights Act 2015 include a short-term right to reject within 30 days and a final right to reject if a repair
or replacement fails. A Borrower who rejects the Vehicle may cease making payments and may be entitled to a refund of
amounts paid, subject to a deduction for use in some cases. These rights apply to the Issuer as assignee of the
Loan Agreements.

Disputes relating to the quality of Vehicles, in particular used Vehicles and electric Vehicles whose batteries
develop faults, are a significant source of complaints to motor finance lenders and of referrals to the Financial
Ombudsman Service. Although the dealer is ultimately responsible to the Originator for defects under the dealer
agreements, the Issuer has no direct rights against the dealer, and its losses from such claims will be recoverable
only through the Seller's obligation to pay Deemed Collections or to repurchase the affected loans.

**The FCA's review of historical motor finance commission arrangements, its industry-wide redress scheme and related
complaints and litigation may result in redress payments, reductions in loan balances and costs which may affect the
Originator and the collections on the loans.**

Following the FCA's ban, with effect from January 2021, on discretionary commission arrangements under which a credit
broker could adjust the interest rate payable by the customer and so increase its commission, a large number of
complaints were made to lenders and to the Financial Ombudsman Service about the non-disclosure of commission on
historical motor finance agreements. In January 2024 the FCA began a review of historical discretionary commission
arrangements and paused the time limits for lenders to respond to related complaints. Following the Supreme Court's
judgment of August 2025 described above, the FCA consulted on, and in 2026 confirmed, an industry-wide consumer redress
scheme under section 404 of FSMA (the "Redress Scheme"). As at the date of this prospectus, the Redress Scheme applies
to regulated motor finance agreements entered into between April 2007 and November 2024 where the arrangement between
the lender and the broker involved a discretionary commission arrangement, commission above the thresholds set by the
FCA in relation to the cost of credit and the amount of the loan, or a tied or exclusive arrangement that was not
adequately disclosed. Under the Redress Scheme, firms are required to identify affected customers, including those who
have not complained, to assess whether redress is due using the methodology and the presumptions set by the FCA, and
to pay redress with interest within the timetable set by the FCA. Aspects of the Redress Scheme, in particular in
relation to older agreements, have been subject to legal challenge, and its implementation, timing and scope may
change.

The portfolio includes loans originated before November 2024 which may fall within the scope of the Redress Scheme if
the relevant commission arrangement meets its criteria. The Originator did not use discretionary commission
arrangements in respect of any loan in the portfolio, because all such loans were originated after the FCA's ban took
effect, but certain loans were introduced by dealers under commission or exclusivity arrangements which may fall
within the other categories of the Redress Scheme, as described in section 8. Where redress is due on a loan which
remains outstanding, the Originator may pay it in cash to the Borrower or apply it as a reduction of the balance
outstanding. Any such reduction will be treated as a Deemed Collection which the Seller must pay to the Issuer.
Borrowers may also seek to set off redress amounts against the instalments due, and claims management companies and
law firms may encourage Borrowers to withhold payments pending the outcome of their claims.

Liability for redress under the Redress Scheme rests with the Originator as the lender which entered into the
agreements and paid the commission, and the Transaction Documents provide that the Originator retains responsibility
for all complaints, redress and costs relating to the origination of the loans. The total cost of redress for the
motor finance industry has been estimated by the FCA at several billion pounds, and a material redress liability, the
costs of administering the Redress Scheme, and the related operational demands may affect the Originator's financial
position, its ability to pay Deemed Collections and to repurchase loans, and its ability to continue as Servicer. The
Originator's assessment of its exposure to the Redress Scheme, and its provisions for it, are described in section 8.
There can be no assurance that those provisions will prove sufficient. Borrowers who are not satisfied with the
outcome of the Redress Scheme may complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service and may bring court proceedings, and
the FCA's review, any future judgment of the courts or any further regulatory action may extend liability to other
forms of commission or to other categories of agreement.

**The FCA's Consumer Duty and other conduct rules may require the Servicer to change its treatment of Borrowers
in ways which reduce the collections on the loans.**

The FCA's Consumer Duty, set out in Principle 12 and PRIN 2A of the FCA Handbook, has applied to products and services
which are open to sale since July 2023 and to closed products since July 2024. It requires firms to act to deliver
good outcomes for retail customers, including by avoiding foreseeable harm, ensuring that products provide fair value
and supporting customers in financial difficulty. The Consumer Duty applies to the Servicer in respect of the loans,
including the loans which are owned by the Issuer, and it may also apply to the Issuer to the extent that it is
treated as a manufacturer or distributor of the loans. The FCA has identified motor finance, the treatment of
borrowers in financial difficulty and the fair value of charges and ancillary products as areas of supervisory focus.

Compliance with the Consumer Duty may require the Servicer to review the fair value of the interest rates, fees and
charges under the Loan Agreements, including the charges for excess mileage and damage on returned PCP Vehicles, to
waive or refund charges, to extend forbearance, to change its collection and repossession practices and to remediate
customers where it identifies that outcomes have been poor. Any such action may reduce or delay the collections of
the Issuer. A failure by the Servicer to comply with the Consumer Duty may also lead to FCA enforcement action or
restrictions on its business. The Transaction Documents permit the Servicer to take action required by law or by the
FCA, even if it reduces collections, and the Issuer will bear the consequences except to the extent that they are
covered by the Seller's obligation to pay Deemed Collections.

**The Issuer and the Servicer are subject to the regulatory regime for consumer credit, and changes in that regime
may adversely affect the loans and the Noteholders.**

Entering into regulated credit agreements as lender, and exercising or having the right to exercise the lender's
rights and duties under them, are regulated activities under FSMA, as are debt collection and debt administration.
The Issuer is not authorised by the FCA, and it relies on the exclusion in the Financial Services and Markets Act
2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001 which permits a person who is not authorised to exercise the lender's rights
under a regulated credit agreement where the activity is carried on by an authorised person with the necessary
permission. The Servicer holds the permissions required to service the loans on behalf of the Issuer. If the Servicer
ceases to hold those permissions, or its appointment is terminated, any substitute servicer must hold them, and if no
suitably authorised servicer is appointed, the Issuer may be unable to exercise its rights under the loans without
itself becoming authorised, which could delay enforcement and collections.

The FCA has wide powers to make rules, issue guidance and take action against regulated firms, including the power to
require redress, impose restrictions on a firm's business and vary its permissions. The rules in CONC on
creditworthiness assessment, arrears and default, forbearance, the treatment of vulnerable customers and the
recovery of goods may be amended at any time, and new rules may apply to existing agreements. HM Treasury has
announced a programme of reform of the CCA, with the aim of replacing many of its detailed requirements, including the
sanctions of unenforceability and loss of interest, with FCA rules. The content and timing of that reform are not yet
known. Any reform may change the rights of Borrowers and creditors under the loans and the remedies available to them,
and the transitional provisions may affect existing agreements, including the loans.

**Borrowers may refer complaints to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which may make awards on the basis of what it
considers fair and reasonable rather than on the basis of the law alone.**

Borrowers are entitled to refer complaints about the Originator, the Servicer and, in respect of the loans, the Issuer
to the Financial Ombudsman Service (the "FOS") under Part XVI of FSMA. The FOS determines complaints by reference to
what is, in its opinion, fair and reasonable in all the circumstances of the case, taking into account the law,
regulations, regulators' rules and guidance, codes of practice and good industry practice, and it is not bound by its
own previous decisions. It may make a money award up to the statutory limit and may direct the firm to take such
steps as it considers just and appropriate. Its determinations are binding on the firm if accepted by the complainant.
As a result, the FOS may order redress in circumstances in which a court would not, and it may reach different
outcomes on similar complaints.

Motor finance has been one of the largest sources of complaints to the FOS in recent years, driven in large part by
commission complaints, many of them submitted by claims management companies and professional representatives. The
handling of commission complaints is now subject to the Redress Scheme, but complaints about affordability, vehicle
quality, voluntary termination, end-of-contract charges and collection practices continue to be referred to the FOS.
A determination of the FOS on a type of complaint may lead to a large number of similar complaints against the
Originator and may have the effect of changing the Servicer's practices across the portfolio. Any award relating to a
loan which reduces the amount payable by the Borrower is a Deemed Collection payable by the Seller, and the Issuer is
exposed to the Seller's ability to pay it.

**Terms of the Loan Agreements may be found to be unfair under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and may not be binding on
Borrowers.**

Part 2 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 provides that a term of a consumer contract which is unfair is not binding on
the consumer. A term is unfair if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the
parties' rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer. The main subject matter and price of the contract
are excluded from the assessment of fairness only if the term is transparent and prominent. The FCA and the
Competition and Markets Authority have powers to challenge the use of unfair terms, and the courts must consider the
fairness of a term of their own motion. Terms which may be at risk include those relating to default charges, the
calculation of amounts payable on termination, charges for excess mileage and damage on return of a Vehicle, and the
creditor's right to vary the agreement. If any such term were held to be unfair, the Issuer may be unable to recover
the amounts payable under it and may have to refund amounts already collected.

**The processing of Borrowers' personal data is subject to data protection law, and a breach of that law may give
rise to claims, fines and restrictions on the use of data.**

The Servicer processes personal data relating to Borrowers in servicing the loans, and the Issuer and the other
transaction parties receive personal data to the extent necessary for the transaction, including the loan-level data
which must be made available to investors under the UK securitisation rules in an anonymised form. The processing of
personal data is subject to the UK General Data Protection Regulation, the Data Protection Act 2018 and the Data (Use
and Access) Act 2025. A breach of those requirements, including a cyber attack or other data security incident at the
Servicer or another transaction party, may result in fines imposed by the Information Commissioner, claims for

extent that they are payable as expenses under section 15.

**The insolvency of the Originator or the Servicer may delay or reduce payments to the Issuer.**

The Originator is a company incorporated in England and Wales and is not a bank or deposit-taker, so it would be subject
to the general insolvency regime of the Insolvency Act 1986 rather than to a special resolution regime. If the
Originator became subject to administration, liquidation or a restructuring plan, or to a moratorium under Part A1 of
the Insolvency Act 1986, the appointed office-holder would control its assets and operations, including the systems,
staff and records needed to service the loans, and the collections held in its accounts. An administrator would
typically be expected to continue servicing the loans for a period, since the Originator's group would continue to own
other motor finance receivables, but there can be no assurance that it would do so effectively or at all. The
insolvency of the Originator would also end the Seller's ability to pay Deemed Collections, to repurchase loans and to
fund the Clean-Up Call, and would leave the Issuer with only unsecured claims for those amounts.

An insolvency office-holder could seek to challenge the sale of the loans to the Issuer, as described in the next risk
factor, or to set aside it as a transaction at an undervalue under section 238 of the Insolvency Act 1986 or a
preference under section 239. The Originator has represented that it is solvent and received full value for the loans,
and the purchase price was determined on an arm's length basis, but there can be no assurance that such a challenge
would not be made. The insolvency of the Originator may also trigger a Servicer Termination Event (as defined below)
and the perfection events described below, with the consequences set out in section 2.4.

**The sale of the loans may be recharacterised as a secured loan, and the equitable assignment of the loans is
subject to the interests of third parties until it is perfected.**

The Originator has sold the loans to the Issuer under the receivables sale agreement (the "Sale Agreement").
The sale of the loans governed by English law and Northern Irish law takes effect as an equitable assignment, and the
sale of the loans governed by Scots law takes effect through a declaration of trust by the Originator in favour of the
Issuer, because an assignation of the Scottish loans would require intimation to each Borrower. The legal title to the
loans remains with the Originator, and the Borrowers will not be notified of the sale, until a perfection event occurs,
including the insolvency of the Originator, the termination of the appointment of the Servicer, or a request by the
Trustee where the security is in jeopardy, as described in section 12 (each a "Perfection Event"). Until perfection,
the Issuer's interest in the loans is subject to any prior equitable interests and to the rights of Borrowers to
discharge their obligations by paying the Originator, the rights of set-off described in section 2.2, and the risk
that a subsequent legal assignee without notice of the Issuer's interest acquires priority. The Issuer will also be
unable to sue Borrowers in its own name without joining the Originator to the proceedings.

Under English law, a court will generally respect the characterisation of a transaction as a sale if the parties have
documented it as such and the terms are consistent with a sale, and will not recharacterise it as a secured loan
merely because the seller retains some of the economic risk or reward. The Originator's retention of the vertical slice,
its right to the Deferred Consideration and its obligations to repurchase loans in breach of warranty are consistent
with a sale in the opinion of Hartwell Quinn LLP, legal advisers to the Issuer and the Originator. A legal opinion is
not binding on a court, however. If the sale were recharacterised as a secured loan, the security would be void
against a liquidator or administrator of the Originator and its creditors for want of registration under Part 25 of
the Companies Act 2006, and the Issuer would be an unsecured creditor of the Originator. In that case Noteholders could
suffer substantial losses.

**The Notes are subject to the UK securitisation rules, and investors which are subject to due diligence
requirements must satisfy themselves that they comply with them.**

The transaction is a securitisation for the purposes of the Securitisation Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/102), the

Rulebook (together, the "UK Securitisation Rules"), which have applied since November 2024. The UK Securitisation Rules

An institutional investor which is subject to the UK Securitisation Rules must, before holding a securitisation
position, verify the matters set out in the rules, including that the risk retention requirement is met, that the
credit-granting criteria of the Originator are sound, and that the required information has been made available, and

or the Trustee makes any representation that the information in this prospectus or in the investor reports is

The UK Securitisation Rules may be amended by the FCA and the PRA, which have stated their intention to keep the regime
under review, including the scope of the due diligence and transparency requirements. Changes to the rules, or to their
interpretation, may affect the regulatory treatment of the Notes and their market value and liquidity.

**Investors in the European Economic Area are subject to the EU securitisation rules, with which the transaction has
not been structured to comply in every respect.**

Institutional investors in the European Economic Area are subject to the due diligence requirements of the EU
Securitisation Regulation as it applies in their jurisdiction. Those requirements apply to investments in

EEA investors should therefore make their own assessment of whether the information available to them is sufficient to

**The Issuer's tax position depends on the securitisation company regime, which may be amended or may cease to apply.**

The Issuer expects to be taxed under the Taxation of Securitisation Companies Regulations 2006 (SI 2006/3296), as

**Payments on the Notes may be made subject to United Kingdom withholding tax, and the Issuer will not pay any

Payments of interest on the Notes are expected to constitute yearly interest which has a United Kingdom source. Because

## 2.4 Counterparty risks

**The Issuer depends on the Servicer to collect the loans, and a disruption in servicing could reduce or delay
collections.**

Under the Servicing Agreement, the Servicer administers the loans on behalf of the Issuer,
collects the instalments by direct debit and other means, manages arrears and defaults, recovers and sells Vehicles,
handles Borrower queries, complaints and end-of-contract options, performs the CCA information requirements and
prepares the monthly data from which the investor reports are produced, as described in section 13. The performance of
the loans depends to a large extent on the quality of that servicing, and in particular on the Servicer's collection
and recovery capabilities. A deterioration in the Servicer's operations, for example because of staff turnover, a
change of systems, a cyber attack, a failure of an outsourced service provider, or the operational demands of the
Redress Scheme, may lead to an increase in arrears and losses and to errors in the information reported to the
Issuer.

The Servicing Agreement sets out the events upon which the appointment of the Servicer may be terminated by the Issuer
or by the Trustee (each a "Servicer Termination Event"), including a failure to transfer collections, a material breach
of its obligations, the loss of its FCA permissions and its insolvency. The termination of the Servicer's appointment
does not take effect until a substitute servicer has been appointed. If the Servicer were to cease to perform its
obligations before a substitute was appointed, collections could be interrupted for a period, the Issuer could be
unable to pay interest on the Notes in full, and the Reserve Fund could be drawn.

**No back-up servicer has been appointed at the Closing Date, and there is no assurance that a substitute servicer
could be appointed promptly or on acceptable terms.**

Pellam Corporate Services Ltd has been appointed as Back-up Servicer Facilitator. Its role, as described in section 13,
is to use reasonable endeavours, following a Servicer Termination Event, to identify and assist the Issuer in
appointing a substitute servicer (a "Back-up Servicer") which holds the necessary FCA permissions and has experience of
servicing UK motor finance receivables. The Back-up Servicer Facilitator is not itself obliged to service the loans, and
it does not hold the permissions to do so. No servicer has agreed in advance to act as substitute, and the Servicer's
systems and data have not been mapped to those of any other servicer.

The number of firms in the United Kingdom with the permissions, systems and experience to service a portfolio of HP and
PCP agreements, including the management of Vehicle recoveries and end-of-contract hand-backs, is limited. A substitute
servicer may require several months to take over the portfolio, may charge a fee higher than the fee paid to the
Servicer, which would rank ahead of interest on the Notes as a senior fee in section 15, and may be unwilling to act in
the market conditions that typically accompany the failure of a servicer. The transfer of servicing may itself lead to
a temporary increase in arrears, as Borrowers change their payment instructions and new direct debit mandates are
established. These factors may reduce collections and the amounts available to pay the Notes.

**The Issuer is exposed to the credit risk of the Account Bank, and amounts held in the issuer accounts are not
protected by deposit insurance.**

All amounts received by the Issuer, including collections transferred by the Servicer and the Reserve Fund, are held
in the issuer accounts with the Account Bank until they are applied on the next Payment Date, as described in
section 14. If the Account Bank became insolvent or subject to resolution, the Issuer would be an unsecured
creditor of it for the balances of those accounts, and the Issuer's deposits would not be protected by the Financial
Services Compensation Scheme. Under the Banking Act 2009, the Bank of England as resolution authority has powers to
transfer the business of a failing bank, to bail in its liabilities, including certain deposits, and to suspend
payment obligations, and the exercise of those powers could delay or reduce the Issuer's access to its funds.

The Transaction Documents require the Account Bank to have ratings at or above levels set by the Rating Agencies
and, if it ceases to have them, to procure its replacement by a suitably rated bank, or a guarantee of its obligations,
within the period stated in section 14. There can be no assurance that a replacement bank willing to act on the terms of
the account bank agreement will be found within that period, or at all, and a failure to replace the Account Bank
may lead to a downgrade of the Notes. The interest paid on the issuer accounts may be lower than the interest payable on
the Notes, which creates negative carry on the balances held in them, including the Reserve Fund.

**Collections are paid first into an account of the Originator, so that the Issuer is exposed to commingling risk
until they are transferred.**

Borrowers pay their instalments by direct debit, card or bank transfer into an account in the name of the Originator
held with a UK clearing bank (the "Collection Account"), together with amounts paid by Borrowers under loans which have
not been sold to the Issuer. The Servicer is required to identify the collections on the loans and transfer them to the
issuer transaction account within the period stated in section 14, and the Originator has declared a trust over the
amounts in the Collection Account which relate to the loans in favour of the Issuer. The Collection Account does not
form part of the Charged Property, and the bank which holds it is not a party to the Transaction Documents.

If the Originator became insolvent while collections were held in the Collection Account, the Issuer's entitlement to
those collections would depend on the effectiveness of the declaration of trust and on the ability of an insolvency
office-holder to identify the amounts belonging to the Issuer. The process of identification and transfer may be
delayed by the insolvency, and if the trust were not effective, or if the funds had been mixed with other money of the
Originator in a way which prevents them from being traced, the Issuer would have only an unsecured claim against the
Originator. In addition, if the bank holding the Collection Account became insolvent, or exercised a right of set-off
against the account, the collections held in it could be lost. Following a Perfection Event, Borrowers will be
instructed to pay directly to an account of the Issuer, but the change of payment instructions may take some time to
implement. The amount at risk at any time is the collections of the relevant period which have not yet been
transferred, which may be substantial relative to the Reserve Fund.

**The Issuer relies on the Cash Manager, the Agent Bank and the Principal Paying Agent for the calculation and payment
of amounts due on the Notes.**

The Cash Manager calculates the Available Funds, the amounts to be drawn from the Reserve Fund, the amounts payable
under section 15 and the results of the trigger tests in section 16, on the basis of the information provided by the
Servicer. The Agent Bank determines the rate of interest on each Class, and the Principal Paying Agent makes payments to the clearing systems. An error in a calculation, a failure of the Servicer to provide
information on time, or an operational failure at the Cash Manager or the Principal Paying Agent, may result in incorrect or late
payments on the Notes. The Transaction Documents provide for the Cash Manager to make estimates in the absence of
information from the Servicer and to reconcile them later, but payments based on estimates may differ from those which
would have been made on the basis of complete information. The replacement of any of these parties following its
insolvency or a breach of its obligations may take time and may increase the costs of the Issuer.

**The Originator's group may experience financial difficulty, which may affect its ability to perform its roles in
the transaction.**

The Originator is a subsidiary of Kestrel Group plc, and its ability to perform its obligations as Seller and Servicer,
to fund repurchases and Deemed Collections, and to continue to originate new loans depends on the financial position of
the group, which in turn depends on its access to warehouse facilities, securitisation markets and other funding. A
disruption in the group's funding, a material redress liability, losses on its other portfolios or the withdrawal of
support from its lenders may affect its ability to continue in business. Kestrel Group plc has not guaranteed any
obligation of the Originator under the Transaction Documents. A decision by the group to cease new lending would not of
itself terminate the Servicer's appointment, but may reduce its investment in servicing capabilities and may affect the
incentives of Borrowers to continue paying, in particular those Borrowers who would otherwise refinance their Balloon
Payments with the Originator at the end of their PCP Agreements.

## 2.5 Macroeconomic and market risks

**Pressure on household finances from the cost of living may increase arrears and defaults.**

The performance of the loans depends on the general economic conditions in the United Kingdom and on their effect on
household incomes and expenditure. In recent years, UK households have experienced a period of high inflation in the
cost of energy, food and housing, followed by rises in interest rates, which reduced real disposable incomes, in
particular for lower-income households and for renters and mortgage borrowers whose fixed rates expired. Although
inflation has fallen from its peak, prices have not returned to their previous levels, and many households have
depleted their savings or increased their borrowing. Increases in taxation, in rents, in the cost of motor insurance
and of vehicle repairs, or in other essential costs may further reduce the ability of Borrowers to meet their
obligations under the loans.

Borrowers with lower credit scores, higher loan-to-value ratios and higher monthly instalments relative to their income
are the most exposed to these pressures. An increase in the number of Borrowers in financial difficulty will increase
arrears, forbearance and defaults, and may also increase the number of voluntary terminations and of complaints about
affordability. The trigger tests in section 16 may lead to a switch to sequential payment in those circumstances, with
the consequences described in section 2.1.

**Changes in interest rates, including the Bank Rate set by the Bank of England, may affect both the Notes and the
Borrowers.**

The interest rate payable on the Notes follows SONIA, which closely follows the Bank Rate set by the Monetary Policy
Committee of the Bank of England. The Bank Rate rose sharply from historically low levels in 2022 and 2023 and has since
been reduced in stages. Its future path is uncertain and will depend on the Bank of England's assessment of inflation
and of the economy. As described in section 2.1, a rise in SONIA increases the interest payable on the Notes without
any increase in the interest received on the fixed rate loans, and so reduces the excess spread. A fall in SONIA
increases excess spread but may also increase prepayments, as Borrowers refinance at lower rates or change their Vehicles
early.

Interest rates also affect the Borrowers. Although the loans bear fixed rates, many Borrowers have other borrowings at
variable rates, including mortgages, credit cards and other consumer loans, whose cost rises with the Bank Rate. Higher
interest rates may therefore increase the financial pressure on Borrowers and the rate of default on the loans, at the
same time as they reduce the excess spread available to absorb losses. Higher interest rates on new motor finance may
also reduce demand for vehicles and so the prices of used Vehicles, and may make it more difficult for Borrowers to
refinance their Balloon Payments at the end of their PCP Agreements, increasing the number of Vehicles returned.

**The used car market may weaken, reducing the value of the Vehicles and the amounts recovered on defaults,
voluntary terminations and hand-backs.**

The amounts which the Issuer recovers from the sale of Vehicles on default, voluntary termination and return at the end
of PCP Agreements depend on the prices of used vehicles in the United Kingdom at the time of sale. Used vehicle prices
have been volatile in recent years. They rose sharply when the supply of new vehicles was restricted by shortages of
semiconductors and other components, and subsequently fell, at different rates for different segments and fuel types,
as the supply of new vehicles recovered and as the prices of new electric vehicles were reduced. Used vehicle prices are
influenced by the supply of new vehicles, manufacturers' pricing and incentives, the volume of vehicles returning from
leasing and PCP contracts, consumer confidence, the cost and availability of finance, fuel and energy prices, government
policy on emissions and taxation, and international trade conditions, including tariffs affecting vehicles and
components.

A fall in used vehicle prices would reduce recoveries on defaulted loans, increase the number and size of Residual Value
Losses on PCP Agreements, increase the incentive of Borrowers to exercise voluntary termination and reduce the equity of
Borrowers in their Vehicles, which may reduce their willingness to keep paying. Because these effects would occur across
the whole portfolio at the same time, a severe fall in used vehicle prices could cause losses on the loans to rise
sharply. The vehicle recovery and remarketing channels used by the Servicer may also become congested in a period of
high volumes of returned and repossessed vehicles, which may delay sales and increase their costs.

**A recession, a rise in unemployment or another economic shock in the United Kingdom may adversely affect the
performance of the loans.**

The United Kingdom's economic performance is affected by domestic factors, including fiscal and monetary policy, and by
international factors, including the economic performance of its trading partners, conflicts and geopolitical
tensions, changes in trade policy and tariffs, the prices of energy and commodities, and disruption to supply chains. A
recession or a rise in unemployment would reduce the ability of Borrowers to pay, and historical experience shows that
the default rate on consumer motor finance rises with unemployment. The automotive sector itself is exposed to the
transition to zero emission vehicles, to competition from new manufacturers and to changes in trade arrangements, and a
downturn in that sector may affect both the employment of Borrowers in regions with a concentration of automotive
manufacturing and the value of the Vehicles. No assurance can be given as to the economic conditions which will prevail
over the life of the Notes.

**Conditions in the asset-backed securities market and in the market for UK motor finance assets may affect the market
value of the Notes, irrespective of the performance of the portfolio.**

The market value of the Notes will be affected by conditions in the credit markets generally and in the market for
asset-backed securities in particular, including the supply of new issues, the demand for asset-backed securities from
banks, insurers and asset managers, changes in the regulatory treatment of securitisation positions, and the market's
perception of the risks of UK consumer credit and motor finance. Negative developments affecting other UK motor finance
lenders or securitisations, such as the Redress Scheme and related litigation, the failure of a lender or servicer, or a
deterioration in the performance of other transactions, may reduce the market value of the Notes even if the portfolio
continues to perform. Noteholders who need to sell their Notes before maturity may therefore realise a loss, and should
not invest unless they are able to bear the economic risk of their investment until the Final Maturity Date.
